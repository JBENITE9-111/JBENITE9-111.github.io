Sanctioning the court that investigates war crimes — while the US is itself a party to a war — is a statement about the rules-based order as much as about the ICC. Allies who back the court will be forced to choose.

Strategic context

US–ICC friction is not new; it spans administrations. What is new is the context: an active US war, an ICC with live files on the region, and an administration comfortable using sanctions as a multi-front tool.

Why it matters

Moscow is the only outside power with open channels to both Tehran and Washington right now — the diesel deal shows the Trump–Putin channel is functional. A genuine Russian mediation would be the first serious de-escalation vector since February. A fake one is still useful intelligence about Russian intentions.

Strategic context

The meeting lands days after Bloomberg reported Russia is supplying missiles to Iran. Arming one party while offering to mediate is a classic great-power move: it buys leverage with both sides. Tehran, for its part, has every reason to appear open to diplomacy while rebuilding its arsenal.

Competing interpretations

Reading one: Moscow genuinely wants a settlement — a long war risks escalation it cannot control. Reading two: mediation is cover for the arms pipeline; Russia wants the war simmering, not solved. Reading three: the offer is aimed at Washington, not Tehran — a signal that Moscow is the indispensable broker, to be cashed in elsewhere (Ukraine, sanctions).
