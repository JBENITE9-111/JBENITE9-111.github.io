Trump says US could end Iran war now — but only with guarantees Tehran never goes nuclear

At Joint Base Andrews, Trump said the seven-month war could end immediately with a no-nuclear-weapons guarantee; he claimed 18 US personnel killed and 159 Iranian ships sunk — figures not independently confirmed — and floated renaming the Strait of Hormuz 'the Trump Strait.'

Updated 11 Oct 2026, 14:05 UTC Verified 16 sources
Full paper https://times-of-the-world.vercel.app/us-israel-iran-war-october/

What happened

Strikes against Iran by the US and Israel began on February 28, 2026, and the conflict continues into October. Al Jazeera's liveblog (Oct 9, 2026) tracks ongoing developments, including Iranian media reports of massive explosions in the Strait of Hormuz. Separately, Trump has said he ruled out an attack on Iran before the November 3 midterm elections, citing 'productive discussions' with Tehran. On October 10, after a second missile attack on Riyadh's King Khalid International Airport, US President Trump told reporters the United States 'may' join Saudi Arabia in striking the Houthis. 'We may. We're going to look at it. We just found out about the recent attack. We will make a decision. We move very quickly,' he said outside the White House. Trump also said he would consider restarting US strikes on Iran — despite his earlier public pledge ruling out an attack on Iran before the November 3 midterm elections (Al Jazeera liveblog, New York Post, Times Now, A News, Mathrubhumi, Times of Israel, Oct 10). On October 11, Trump set a public end-condition at Joint Base Andrews after a campaign rally in Tennessee: the US 'could leave right now,' he said, 'but we have to guarantee that they can't have it [a nuclear weapon] in the future. And we'll get that.' He claimed 18 American personnel have been killed in the nearly seven-month conflict, said he had met families of the fallen at Dover Air Force Base, and claimed US operations had sunk 159 Iranian ships since late February and destroyed much of Iran's navy, air force, radar systems and manufacturing infrastructure. He also said Tehran would 'probably do a little bit extra before the election, thinking it's going to hurt us,' and floated renaming the Strait of Hormuz 'the Trump Strait' (IANS, lokmattimes, Oct 11). The casualty count, ship-sinking figure and capability claims are Trump's own assertions and have not been independently confirmed.

Why it matters

A war in its eighth month now has a political calendar attached: the US midterms. Trump's stated pause — real or rhetorical — creates a window in which diplomacy (Putin mediation, Gaza ceasefire threads, diesel deals) is suddenly load-bearing. It also sets up a cliff edge: what happens after November 3.

Strategic context

The war began Feb 28, 2026 and has widened across the region: Saudi-led operations in Yemen, Houthi-linked strikes reaching Riyadh, IRGC action in the Strait of Hormuz. Energy markets, shipping insurance, and regional alliances are all downstream of this conflict.

Competing interpretations

Reading one: the pre-midterm pause is genuine — Washington wants de-escalation and is working channels. Reading two: it is electoral management — no new wars before November 3, then reassess. Reading three: Tehran reads the pause as a sign of exhaustion and will use the window to rebuild (see the missile-supply reporting), making the post-midterm cliff sharper.

Trump says US 'may' join Saudi strikes on Houthis after second Riyadh airport attack

A second missile strike on Riyadh's King Khalid airport — hitting the terminals 3–4 complex — forced evacuations and a shutdown; embassies warned citizens off Saudi airspace, carriers canceled flights, and Trump left the door open to US military involvement.

Updated 11 Oct 2026, 14:05 UTC Verified 32 sources
Full paper https://times-of-the-world.vercel.app/yemen-houthis-hormuz-strait/

What happened

A Saudi-led coalition has launched a large-scale operation against the Houthis in Yemen, according to Al Jazeera reporting (Oct 9, 2026). The operation follows a Houthi-linked attack on Riyadh's King Khalid airport that killed three Saudi nationals. In a separate development, Iran's IRGC struck the LPG carrier NV Sunshine near the Strait of Hormuz. The NV Sunshine claim is now disputed: the vessel's owner, NVTrans, told AAP on Oct 10 that the M/T NV SUNSHINE 'was not attacked, no fire occurred in its engine room, and the vessel and all crew members remain safe, with the vessel operating normally' — directly contradicting the IRGC's claim of striking it for taking an 'illegal route.' Separately, Britain's UKMTO maritime agency reported Oct 9 that a ship 13 nautical miles off the UAE's Al Jazeera Al Hamra was struck by a projectile causing a fire; British security firm Vanguard identified that vessel as the Panama-flagged crude tanker GEM NO.2 (NDTV, Oct 10). Iranian state media also reported (Times Now, Oct 10) that an unidentified oil tanker exploded after hitting a naval mine in the strait. On Saturday, October 10, a second missile attack hit Riyadh's King Khalid International Airport — the third attack on the airport this week. The missile struck a complex housing terminals 3 and 4, which handle domestic flights, according to regional officials cited by Mathrubhumi. The airport was evacuated and operations temporarily suspended; Flightradar24 data showed flights to Riyadh diverted or canceled. Saudi authorities confirmed 'a number of injuries' with the count undetermined. Airlines canceled services: Kuwait Airways canceled flights to and from Riyadh; Etihad and Qatar Airways canceled multiple flights, some through Monday; Lufthansa halted services through Oct 16; Air India, IndiGo and Akasa suspended or canceled routes earlier in the week. The US embassy in Riyadh 'strongly recommends all US citizens avoid this airport and surrounding areas'; the UK, Irish, Canadian and German embassies issued similar warnings, and EASA advised commercial airlines to bypass affected Saudi airspace. In response, Trump told reporters the US may join Saudi Arabia in striking the Houthis: 'We may. We're going to look at it. We just found out about the recent attack. We will make a decision. We move very quickly' (Al Jazeera liveblog, NY Post, Times Now, A News, Mathrubhumi, Times of Israel, Oct 10). The Houthis did not immediately claim Saturday's attack; they had claimed responsibility for previous strikes this week and declared Saudi airspace a military-operations zone. On Sunday the shutdown persisted: a senior aviation source told AFP Riyadh airport operations were still suspended at dawn, Flightradar24 reported the closure extended to 21:00 UTC, and Saudi authorities said roughly 260 flights were cancelled and 219 delayed (Gulf News, livemint). India "strongly condemned" the "dastardly" attack and Pakistan's prime minister called the strikes "heinous," both reaffirming Saudi sovereignty (livemint, Oct 11). The Houthis escalated rhetorically: political bureau member Mohammed al-Frah warned of a "terrifying" new phase against infrastructure "far beyond airports and oil facilities" (ABNA24 republication of his Oct 10 X post; single Iran-aligned source). On the ground around Bab al-Mandab, a Saudi-backed coalition operation launched Friday intensified — military sources told Dawn at least 63 were killed in 24 hours; the coalition claimed 130+ Houthi targets destroyed Saturday while the Houthis said they repelled the advance and hold Bab al-Mandab, Mayun Island and the western coast. On Sunday, CNN reported Saturday's was the third missile strike this week — the kingdom's deadliest in more than a decade — with no Saudi warning issued and 600+ flights canceled. Senior Houthi official Nasr al-Din Amer appeared to claim the attack on X ('We shut down Riyadh international airport'). Coalition spokesman Turki Al Maliki called it 'a full-fledged war crime' and vowed a decisive response; Houthi military spokesman Yahya Saree formally warned airlines off Saudi airports and airspace — 'Airport for airport, blockade for blockade and escalation for escalation' — and declared all of Saudi Arabia except Mecca and Medina potential military theaters. UN chief Guterres said he was 'deeply shocked.' Iran's Deputy FM Gharibabadi warned Iran's response to any new military action would differ from previous responses. The disruption is spilling into daily life: Riyadh's education ministry ordered schools across the capital to a week of remote learning — the second such move in under a month — and Emirates joined Lufthansa in canceling Riyadh services (The Times, UK, Oct 11). UK Defence Secretary Wes Streeting called the strike a 'dangerous escalation' but said Britain is not looking at offensive operations 'at this stage'; he confirmed the UK is already providing 'defensive' air-to-air refuelling support to Saudi Arabia and said he had discussed further British support with his Saudi counterpart. The attacks drew condemnation from Secretary of State Rubio, EU Commission President Ursula von der Leyen and UN chief Guterres (same). The Times further reports Saudi Arabia has leaned on Pakistan and Turkey — which signed a defence pact earlier this year — for support, along with France; all three pledged defensive support but ruled out sending forces to Yemen, while Syria has sent drone operators to government-controlled Aden, per two sources. AP reported Sunday that Thursday's attack damaged three planes at the airport, per two anonymous regional officials, with the Saudi carrier confirming one damaged aircraft on the ground; Saudi aviation suspended operations through midnight, and five governments (US, UK, Germany, Australia, Canada) warned citizens away from the airport. The Saudi foreign minister spoke Saturday with Oman's foreign minister about diplomatic de-escalation. Fresh attention returned to AP's earlier reporting that Saudi Arabia is running low on missile interceptors and sought air-defense support from France, Britain, Pakistan and Egypt, with US interceptor stocks also depleted during the Iran war. Early Sunday, the Saudi-led coalition said it intercepted and destroyed a Houthi ballistic missile over Al-Kharj governorate, southeast of Riyadh (Xinhua/NAMPA; AP-carried reports; EA WorldView). The Houthi-run foreign ministry warned that further Saudi military action in Yemen would draw a stronger response under 'the principle of reciprocity' (tribune.com.pk, single carrier).

What remains disputed

The precise objectives and scope of the coalition operation are not public. Responsibility attribution for the airport attack and the NV Sunshine strike follows the cited reporting; independent confirmation is pending. The earlier NY Post/NYT figure of 'at least 12 killed, 50 injured' is superseded by Saudi GACA's official toll (12 killed, 309 wounded) — see corrected entry in 'what changed.' Whether the NV Sunshine was struck at all is now contested: the IRGC claims it struck the vessel for taking an 'illegal route'; the vessel's owner says no attack occurred. Two distinct Hormuz tanker incidents are being conflated in some reporting — the NV Sunshine claim and the GEM NO.2/UKMTO incident off the UAE coast. The Houthis' declaration of all Saudi airspace as a military-operations zone is reported by a single outlet (travelerstoday) and is unattributed. The Yemeni government spokesman's claim that 1,150 Houthi fighters were "neutralised" and 180+ vehicles destroyed in 1,900 strikes across three fronts in 24 hours (Majed al-Nuzaili via X, re-carried by Xinhua/lokmattimes) is single-source, extraordinary, and directly contradicts Houthi claims of repelling the offensive — the newsroom is not carrying it until an independent source confirms. Similarly, al-Frah's "terrifying new phase" language is attributed messaging from a political-bureau post republished by the Iran-aligned ABNA24; it is not treated as evidence of an operational plan. Houthi media's report of two Saudi strikes on Sanaa governorate Sunday is single-source and unconfirmed by Riyadh. A Western diplomat told AFP the official 309-injured figure appears incomplete — injury accounting remains disputed. The Sanaa airport attack report comes from Houthi-run media with no coalition comment; the Harf Sufyan strikes are Houthi-affiliated media reports. The Taiz strikes are the Yemeni government's claim alone. Al-Omeisy's midterms analysis is expert assessment, not an official US position.

Why it matters

Two chokepoints, one escalation. The Red Sea and the Strait of Hormuz together carry a dominant share of the world's seaborne oil and LNG. The NV Sunshine strike is the most direct Iranian action against commercial shipping in the strait in this war cycle — and the Saudi operation signals Riyadh is done absorbing Houthi strikes without response.

Strategic context

The Houthis have struck at Saudi territory before, but an airport attack killing three nationals is a qualitative escalation — and Riyadh's answer is a large-scale operation rather than a limited retaliation. The IRGC move in Hormuz runs parallel to Iranian media reports of explosions in the strait, tightening the maritime pressure campaign.

Competing interpretations

Reading one: coordinated Iran–Houthi escalation to raise the cost of the war for the Gulf states. Reading two: opportunistic but separate moves — Riyadh's Yemen operation was planned regardless, and the Hormuz strike is Tehran's independent signal. Reading three: a deliberate pre-November escalation ladder by the Iran axis to extract concessions while Washington is politically constrained.
