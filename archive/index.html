Trump and Putin struck a diesel deal to blunt Iran-war fuel prices

Over 300,000 metric tons of Russian fuel would flow under a temporary US sanctions lift, easing the price shock from the war on Iran — but analysts doubt it will be enough.

Updated 11 Oct 2026, 13:40 UTC Verified 21 sources
Full paper https://times-of-the-world.vercel.app/diesel-deal-iran-war/

What happened

The Trump administration and Russia have struck an arrangement to supply more than 300,000 metric tons of Russian diesel to the US and global markets, according to Haaretz reporting published October 10, 2026. The deal involves a temporary US lift of sanctions on Russian fuel products. Diesel prices fell after the announcement. Further details published Oct 9–10: Trump said on Truth Social that Russia would supply more than 300,000 metric tons of diesel immediately, 500,000 tonnes in November, another 1 million tonnes after that, and a further 3 million tonnes 'within a short period of time' depending on the condition of Russia's diesel refineries. The US Treasury/OFAC formally issued a license authorizing transactions related to the sale, delivery, offloading, or importation of Russian-origin diesel fuel through 12:01 am eastern daylight time, April 7, 2027 — the license does not authorize debits to accounts of Russia's central bank, national wealth fund, or finance ministry. The Kremlin confirmed the arrangement, saying Russia 'reaffirmed its readiness to supply oil and petroleum products to US and global markets,' with Putin expressing confidence it would positively impact the global economy. Trump claimed the US has 'total control' of the Strait of Hormuz and predicted prices would come down 'in record numbers, and fast.' Ukrainian President Zelenskyy condemned the move, saying it was 'an investment in war that needs to end, not continue,' warning Russia would 'repay' the diesel 'with further terror and perfidy.' US Senate Democrats also objected. The Friday call between Trump and Putin lasted an hour and a half and was 'very substantive and friendly,' Kremlin foreign policy aide Yuri Ushakov told Interfax on October 10 — 'neither wanted to be the first to hang up,' he said, adding they 'hung up simultaneously, literally at the same time.' The Kremlin said most of the conversation focused on efforts to resolve the war in Ukraine, and Putin downplayed the likelihood of three-way peace talks resuming soon. Separately, negotiators from the US, Ukraine and European countries held talks in Miami on October 9 aimed at reaching agreement on a unified peace proposal in the coming weeks that could be presented to Russia (Reuters, Oct 10). On October 10, Trump sharpened his public framing of the Friday call: he said Putin "would like to see a settlement, strongly" and that "Russia would like to make a deal... but for whatever reason, Zelenskyy does not want to make a deal," repeating his call for Ukraine to hold an election (Al Arabiya/AFP, Indian Express, Oct 11). EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas pushed back directly, saying suspending diesel sanctions "provides Moscow with more revenues to wage war" and that Europe "will not" ease pressure; she said EU foreign ministers intend on Monday to approve the biggest Russia sanctions package since the war began (Kallas X post; corroborated by multiple outlets). On the ground, the refinery war continued: Ukraine struck the Samara LVDS station for a second time and hit the Novoshakhtinsk/NZNP oil terminal near Rostov-on-Don overnight October 10, with the Rostov governor confirming the fire (RBC-Ukraine, Ukrainska Pravda, Kyiv Post); a senior Ukrainian official told the FT the refinery strikes will continue despite the diesel deal. Axios reported via Shafaq News (Oct 10) that Trump moved on the deal after Zelenskyy ignored six US requests to halt strikes on Russian refineries — Trump's envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner had flagged the planned Putin call and the diesel sanctions lift during eight hours of Miami talks on Friday; Zelenskyy countered that Ukraine would stop striking refineries if Russia halts attacks on Ukraine's power plants. The OFAC instrument was identified as General License 135, issued October 9. Russia's deputy prime minister for energy said volumes could reach 3 million tons per month (Le Monde). Trump told reporters Oct 10: 'I think it's time for Ukraine to get a new president... I suggest they get a new leader who can make a deal' — adding 'We told them, do whatever you want, but don't hit the diesel fuel because there's a big problem, a big shortage' (USA Today). CNN calculated the announced volumes total about 6 million barrels — roughly a day and a half of US demand (EIA) — and cited former US officials doubting Putin has that much diesel to sell; Kyiv claimed strikes on five refineries this week. AP reported Russian strikes killed at least 11 in Zaporizhzhia on Saturday, three of them children, with 60+ killed across Ukraine in four days. Newly ingested on-the-record remarks from President Zelenskyy (made Oct 9, after meeting a US congressional delegation in Kyiv): he called the sanctions relief "absolutely terrible" and a "happy birthday present for Putin" that would give Russia "billions more" to continue the war, and said Ukraine's negotiating team in the US was "simply being used as a cover" while the deal was struck — "a weak decision by strong partners" (New Voice of Ukraine, Ukrainska Pravda). Zelenskyy further suggested Trump's envoys were using the agreement to "buy" a visit to Moscow ("it looks that you are buying this meeting"), while stressing Ukraine still needs US mediation to stop the war. On Sunday, Zelenskyy publicly offered a reciprocal halt: Ukraine would stop striking Russian oil refineries if Russia stopped striking Ukrainian energy infrastructure, saying such a halt was 'feasible... today' but accusing Putin of unwillingness (Kyiv Post, NDTV, Malay Mail/AFP). He directly linked the diesel sanctions relief to Saturday's five glide-bomb strikes on Zaporizhzhia residential buildings — 'Russia said thank you for the lifting of sanctions' — with rescue operations reporting 20–23 dead including children (BusinessWorld, Kyiv Post, NDTV via AFP; toll figures vary by source). Zelenskyy also reported a Kharkiv medical facility and a Slovyansk apartment block struck, roughly 1,450 aerial bombs, 75 missiles and 1,640+ drones used by Russia this week, and fresh DTEK power outages in Kyiv; he said Sunday talks with European leaders would focus on air-defense packages.

What remains disputed

Analysts cited in the reporting doubt the volume will be enough to materially calm fuel markets. The exact duration of the sanctions carve-out, the delivery timeline, and how the fuel will be routed into US supply chains remain unclear. Whether this marks a broader policy shift toward Russia or a one-off wartime measure is not established. Russia's deputy PM for energy floated volumes up to 3 million tons per month — unconfirmed and above Trump's stated figures; whether the deal covers ~6 million barrels (~1.5 days of US demand per EIA via CNN) or a larger flow is unsettled. Zelenskyy's allegation that Ukraine's negotiating team was used as a 'cover' — and his suggestion that the deal was struck to 'buy' a Moscow visit for Trump's envoys — is his claim alone; US officials have not responded to it in this newsroom's sourcing, and no independent account of the deal's internal timing exists publicly. Zaporizhzhia's Saturday toll is reported at 20 dead (Kyiv Post, Oct 11), 23 dead including four children (Zelenskyy, Oct 11), and 'at least 11' (AP, Oct 10) — the figures reflect different reporting times and sources, not a single settled count. Russia's 'partial lifting' of its diesel export ban is Moscow's claim alone. Whether Zelenskyy's energy-truce offer draws a Russian response is unknown.

Why it matters

Energy is the second front of the Iran war. With airstrikes disrupting supply routes and insurance markets jittery since February 2026, fuel prices feed directly into inflation — a political problem months before the US midterms. A US–Russia energy deal, even temporary, would have been unthinkable a year ago; its arrival signals how acute the price pressure has become.

Strategic context

Since strikes on Iran began on February 28, 2026, global diesel and distillate markets have tightened. Russia — under Western sanctions since 2022 — remains one of the world's largest diesel exporters, with volumes effectively locked out of Western markets. The Trump administration has publicly pursued a pragmatic, deal-driven approach to Moscow throughout 2026, making energy the latest testing ground.

Competing interpretations

One reading: this is pure price management — a tactical wartime fix with no strategic meaning. Another: it is the thin end of sanctions normalization, giving Moscow a sanctioned-product channel back into Western markets. A third, more skeptical reading: the volume is symbolic, designed to move headlines and futures prices rather than physical supply. The evidence so far supports the first reading, but the structure of the sanctions carve-out will be the tell.

Trump says US 'may' join Saudi strikes on Houthis after second Riyadh airport attack

A second missile strike on Riyadh's King Khalid airport — hitting the terminals 3–4 complex — forced evacuations and a shutdown; embassies warned citizens off Saudi airspace, carriers canceled flights, and Trump left the door open to US military involvement.

Updated 11 Oct 2026, 13:05 UTC Verified 32 sources
Full paper https://times-of-the-world.vercel.app/yemen-houthis-hormuz-strait/

What happened

A Saudi-led coalition has launched a large-scale operation against the Houthis in Yemen, according to Al Jazeera reporting (Oct 9, 2026). The operation follows a Houthi-linked attack on Riyadh's King Khalid airport that killed three Saudi nationals. In a separate development, Iran's IRGC struck the LPG carrier NV Sunshine near the Strait of Hormuz. The NV Sunshine claim is now disputed: the vessel's owner, NVTrans, told AAP on Oct 10 that the M/T NV SUNSHINE 'was not attacked, no fire occurred in its engine room, and the vessel and all crew members remain safe, with the vessel operating normally' — directly contradicting the IRGC's claim of striking it for taking an 'illegal route.' Separately, Britain's UKMTO maritime agency reported Oct 9 that a ship 13 nautical miles off the UAE's Al Jazeera Al Hamra was struck by a projectile causing a fire; British security firm Vanguard identified that vessel as the Panama-flagged crude tanker GEM NO.2 (NDTV, Oct 10). Iranian state media also reported (Times Now, Oct 10) that an unidentified oil tanker exploded after hitting a naval mine in the strait. On Saturday, October 10, a second missile attack hit Riyadh's King Khalid International Airport — the third attack on the airport this week. The missile struck a complex housing terminals 3 and 4, which handle domestic flights, according to regional officials cited by Mathrubhumi. The airport was evacuated and operations temporarily suspended; Flightradar24 data showed flights to Riyadh diverted or canceled. Saudi authorities confirmed 'a number of injuries' with the count undetermined. Airlines canceled services: Kuwait Airways canceled flights to and from Riyadh; Etihad and Qatar Airways canceled multiple flights, some through Monday; Lufthansa halted services through Oct 16; Air India, IndiGo and Akasa suspended or canceled routes earlier in the week. The US embassy in Riyadh 'strongly recommends all US citizens avoid this airport and surrounding areas'; the UK, Irish, Canadian and German embassies issued similar warnings, and EASA advised commercial airlines to bypass affected Saudi airspace. In response, Trump told reporters the US may join Saudi Arabia in striking the Houthis: 'We may. We're going to look at it. We just found out about the recent attack. We will make a decision. We move very quickly' (Al Jazeera liveblog, NY Post, Times Now, A News, Mathrubhumi, Times of Israel, Oct 10). What remains disputed

The precise objectives and scope of the coalition operation are not public. Responsibility attribution for the airport attack and the NV Sunshine strike follows the cited reporting; independent confirmation is pending. The earlier NY Post/NYT figure of 'at least 12 killed, 50 injured' is superseded by Saudi GACA's official toll (12 killed, 309 wounded) — see corrected entry in 'what changed.' Whether the NV Sunshine was struck at all is now contested: the IRGC claims it struck the vessel for taking an 'illegal route'; the vessel's owner says no attack occurred. Two distinct Hormuz tanker incidents are being conflated in some reporting — the NV Sunshine claim and the GEM NO.2/UKMTO incident off the UAE coast. The Houthis' declaration of all Saudi airspace as a military-operations zone is reported by a single outlet (travelerstoday) and is unattributed. The Yemeni government spokesman's claim that 1,150 Houthi fighters were "neutralised" and 180+ vehicles destroyed in 1,900 strikes across three fronts in 24 hours (Majed al-Nuzaili via X, re-carried by Xinhua/lokmattimes) is single-source, extraordinary, and directly contradicts Houthi claims of repelling the offensive — the newsroom is not carrying it until an independent source confirms. Similarly, al-Frah's "terrifying new phase" language is attributed messaging from a political-bureau post republished by the Iran-aligned ABNA24; it is not treated as evidence of an operational plan. Houthi media's report of two Saudi strikes on Sanaa governorate Sunday is single-source and unconfirmed by Riyadh. A Western diplomat told AFP the official 309-injured figure appears incomplete — injury accounting remains disputed.

Why it matters

Two chokepoints, one escalation. The Red Sea and the Strait of Hormuz together carry a dominant share of the world's seaborne oil and LNG. The NV Sunshine strike is the most direct Iranian action against commercial shipping in the strait in this war cycle — and the Saudi operation signals Riyadh is done absorbing Houthi strikes without response.

Strategic context

The Houthis have struck at Saudi territory before, but an airport attack killing three nationals is a qualitative escalation — and Riyadh's answer is a large-scale operation rather than a limited retaliation. The IRGC move in Hormuz runs parallel to Iranian media reports of explosions in the strait, tightening the maritime pressure campaign.

Competing interpretations

Reading one: coordinated Iran–Houthi escalation to raise the cost of the war for the Gulf states. Reading two: opportunistic but separate moves — Riyadh's Yemen operation was planned regardless, and the Hormuz strike is Tehran's independent signal. Reading three: a deliberate pre-November escalation ladder by the Iran axis to extract concessions while Washington is politically constrained.
