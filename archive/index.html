Trump and Putin struck a diesel deal to blunt Iran-war fuel prices

Over 300,000 metric tons of Russian fuel would flow under a temporary US sanctions lift, easing the price shock from the war on Iran — but analysts doubt it will be enough.

Updated 11 Oct 2026, 16:35 UTC Verified 33 sources
Full paper https://times-of-the-world.vercel.app/diesel-deal-iran-war/

What happened

The Trump administration and Russia have struck an arrangement to supply more than 300,000 metric tons of Russian diesel to the US and global markets, according to Haaretz reporting published October 10, 2026. The deal involves a temporary US lift of sanctions on Russian fuel products. Diesel prices fell after the announcement. Further details published Oct 9–10: Trump said on Truth Social that Russia would supply more than 300,000 metric tons of diesel immediately, 500,000 tonnes in November, another 1 million tonnes after that, and a further 3 million tonnes 'within a short period of time' depending on the condition of Russia's diesel refineries. The US Treasury/OFAC formally issued a license authorizing transactions related to the sale, delivery, offloading, or importation of Russian-origin diesel fuel through 12:01 am eastern daylight time, April 7, 2027 — the license does not authorize debits to accounts of Russia's central bank, national wealth fund, or finance ministry. The Kremlin confirmed the arrangement, saying Russia 'reaffirmed its readiness to supply oil and petroleum products to US and global markets,' with Putin expressing confidence it would positively impact the global economy. Trump claimed the US has 'total control' of the Strait of Hormuz and predicted prices would come down 'in record numbers, and fast.' Ukrainian President Zelenskyy condemned the move, saying it was 'an investment in war that needs to end, not continue,' warning Russia would 'repay' the diesel 'with further terror and perfidy.' US Senate Democrats also objected. The Friday call between Trump and Putin lasted an hour and a half and was 'very substantive and friendly,' Kremlin foreign policy aide Yuri Ushakov told Interfax on October 10 — 'neither wanted to be the first to hang up,' he said, adding they 'hung up simultaneously, literally at the same time.' The Kremlin said most of the conversation focused on efforts to resolve the war in Ukraine, and Putin downplayed the likelihood of three-way peace talks resuming soon. Separately, negotiators from the US, Ukraine and European countries held talks in Miami on October 9 aimed at reaching agreement on a unified peace proposal in the coming weeks that could be presented to Russia (Reuters, Oct 10). On October 10, Trump sharpened his public framing of the Friday call: he said Putin "would like to see a settlement, strongly" and that "Russia would like to make a deal... but for whatever reason, Zelenskyy does not want to make a deal," repeating his call for Ukraine to hold an election (Al Arabiya/AFP, Indian Express, Oct 11). EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas pushed back directly, saying suspending diesel sanctions "provides Moscow with more revenues to wage war" and that Europe "will not" ease pressure; she said EU foreign ministers intend on Monday to approve the biggest Russia sanctions package since the war began (Kallas X post; corroborated by multiple outlets). On the ground, the refinery war continued: Ukraine struck the Samara LVDS station for a second time and hit the Novoshakhtinsk/NZNP oil terminal near Rostov-on-Don overnight October 10, with the Rostov governor confirming the fire (RBC-Ukraine, Ukrainska Pravda, Kyiv Post); a senior Ukrainian official told the FT the refinery strikes will continue despite the diesel deal. Axios reported via Shafaq News (Oct 10) that Trump moved on the deal after Zelenskyy ignored six US requests to halt strikes on Russian refineries — Trump's envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner had flagged the planned Putin call and the diesel sanctions lift during eight hours of Miami talks on Friday; Zelenskyy countered that Ukraine would stop striking refineries if Russia halts attacks on Ukraine's power plants. The OFAC instrument was identified as General License 135, issued October 9. Russia's deputy prime minister for energy said volumes could reach 3 million tons per month (Le Monde). Trump told reporters Oct 10: 'I think it's time for Ukraine to get a new president... I suggest they get a new leader who can make a deal' — adding 'We told them, do whatever you want, but don't hit the diesel fuel because there's a big problem, a big shortage' (USA Today). CNN calculated the announced volumes total about 6 million barrels — roughly a day and a half of US demand (EIA) — and cited former US officials doubting Putin has that much diesel to sell; Kyiv claimed strikes on five refineries this week. AP reported Russian strikes killed at least 11 in Zaporizhzhia on Saturday, three of them children, with 60+ killed across Ukraine in four days. Newly ingested on-the-record remarks from President Zelenskyy (made Oct 9, after meeting a US congressional delegation in Kyiv): he called the sanctions relief "absolutely terrible" and a "happy birthday present for Putin" that would give Russia "billions more" to continue the war, and said Ukraine's negotiating team in the US was "simply being used as a cover" while the deal was struck — "a weak decision by strong partners" (New Voice of Ukraine, Ukrainska Pravda). Zelenskyy further suggested Trump's envoys were using the agreement to "buy" a visit to Moscow ("it looks that you are buying this meeting"), while stressing Ukraine still needs US mediation to stop the war. On Sunday, Zelenskyy publicly offered a reciprocal halt: Ukraine would stop striking Russian oil refineries if Russia stopped striking Ukrainian energy infrastructure, saying such a halt was 'feasible... today' but accusing Putin of unwillingness (Kyiv Post, NDTV, Malay Mail/AFP). He directly linked the diesel sanctions relief to Saturday's five glide-bomb strikes on Zaporizhzhia residential buildings — 'Russia said thank you for the lifting of sanctions' — with rescue operations reporting 20–23 dead including children (BusinessWorld, Kyiv Post, NDTV via AFP; toll figures vary by source). Zelenskyy also reported a Kharkiv medical facility and a Slovyansk apartment block struck, roughly 1,450 aerial bombs, 75 missiles and 1,640+ drones used by Russia this week, and fresh DTEK power outages in Kyiv; he said Sunday talks with European leaders would focus on air-defense packages. On Sunday evening the European backlash hardened with concrete detail: roughly 1,600 new sanctions listings — 827 companies and 744 individuals, per ukrmedia.news and the Kyiv Independent — were unanimously agreed by EU ambassadors on Oct 7 and are set for adoption by EU foreign ministers meeting in Luxembourg on Monday, Oct 12 (modern.az, iz.ru, censor.net, Euromaidan Press). German government spokesman Stefan Cornelius said Berlin adheres to all previous sanctions decisions and is working with European partners on new ones (Welt/Reuters via LIGA.net), while Finnish PM Petteri Orpo called the diesel deal 'a terrible disappointment' and proposed suspending tourist visas for Russians (same). The European Commission said Europe 'knows all too well the hidden costs of over-reliance on Russian hydrocarbons' (SBS). Kallas put the recent civilian cost at ~80 Ukrainian civilians killed in two days of Russian strikes, alongside stepped-up Russian hybrid attacks against Europe (SBS, Euromaidan Press). Trump kept up the pressure on Zelenskyy en route to a Tennessee rally, saying: 'You've lost 30% of your country and you've had over a million people killed,' and 'I said to him: do what you want, don't hit refineries' — 'He'd better damned well stop' (NY Post, Oct 10). The announced diesel volumes total 4.8 million metric tons under Trump's stated schedule (LIGA.net). AFP's carried tally for Saturday's Zaporizhzhia attack was updated to at least 21 dead, including four children (Straits Times). On Sunday morning, Trump announced on Truth Social that Russia and Ukraine had agreed to an “energy ceasefire” effective immediately — “Both parties have agreed. Do not break it!” — carried by CNN, the New York Post and Fox News within the hour. The announcement landed on top of Zelenskyy's reciprocal-halt offer but its central claim was immediately in doubt: Ukrainian officials told CNN the announcement was news to them, even as one source close to Zelensky said Ukraine would agree if Russia does. In an ABC News interview earlier Sunday, Zelensky said Ukraine was open to the move and would stop attacking Russia's diesel infrastructure if Russia stopped attacking Ukraine. No Russian confirmation was on record at the time, and CNN said it was not clear what led to the Sunday announcement.

What remains disputed

Analysts cited in the reporting doubt the volume will be enough to materially calm fuel markets. The exact duration of the sanctions carve-out, the delivery timeline, and how the fuel will be routed into US supply chains remain unclear. Whether this marks a broader policy shift toward Russia or a one-off wartime measure is not established. Russia's deputy PM for energy floated volumes up to 3 million tons per month — unconfirmed and above Trump's stated figures; whether the deal covers ~6 million barrels (~1.5 days of US demand per EIA via CNN) or a larger flow is unsettled. Zelenskyy's allegation that Ukraine's negotiating team was used as a 'cover' — and his suggestion that the deal was struck to 'buy' a Moscow visit for Trump's envoys — is his claim alone; US officials have not responded to it in this newsroom's sourcing, and no independent account of the deal's internal timing exists publicly. Zaporizhzhia's Saturday toll is reported at 20 dead (Kyiv Post, Oct 11), 23 dead including four children (Zelenskyy, Oct 11), and 'at least 11' (AP, Oct 10) — the figures reflect different reporting times and sources, not a single settled count. Russia's 'partial lifting' of its diesel export ban is Moscow's claim alone. Whether Zelenskyy's energy-truce offer draws a Russian response is unknown. Updated AFP-carried tally for the Saturday Zaporizhzhia attack: at least 21 dead, including four children (Straits Times, Oct 11) — alongside 20 dead (Kyiv Post), 23 dead incl. four children (Zelenskyy), and at least 11 (AP, Oct 10). Trump's claim that “both parties have agreed” to the energy ceasefire is contested — CNN-cited Ukrainian officials said the announcement was news to them, no Russian confirmation was on record at the time, and CNN said the genesis of the Sunday announcement was unclear. Zelenskyy's 60-day total-ceasefire-for-elections offer is conditional on Putin's agreement — no Russian response to either the energy ceasefire or the election proposal was on record at ingest; Rosatom's expectation of a repair agreement is a Russian corporate expectation, not a ceasefire commitment.

Why it matters

Energy is the second front of the Iran war. With airstrikes disrupting supply routes and insurance markets jittery since February 2026, fuel prices feed directly into inflation — a political problem months before the US midterms. A US–Russia energy deal, even temporary, would have been unthinkable a year ago; its arrival signals how acute the price pressure has become.

Strategic context

Since strikes on Iran began on February 28, 2026, global diesel and distillate markets have tightened. Russia — under Western sanctions since 2022 — remains one of the world's largest diesel exporters, with volumes effectively locked out of Western markets. The Trump administration has publicly pursued a pragmatic, deal-driven approach to Moscow throughout 2026, making energy the latest testing ground.

Competing interpretations

One reading: this is pure price management — a tactical wartime fix with no strategic meaning. Another: it is the thin end of sanctions normalization, giving Moscow a sanctioned-product channel back into Western markets. A third, more skeptical reading: the volume is symbolic, designed to move headlines and futures prices rather than physical supply. The evidence so far supports the first reading, but the structure of the sanctions carve-out will be the tell.
