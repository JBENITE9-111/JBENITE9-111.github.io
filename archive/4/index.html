One year on, Gaza's promised 'day after' has not arrived — AP

A year after the US-brokered ceasefire took effect, all captives are home but Israel controls ~70% of Gaza, reconstruction has not begun, and strikes continue — medical sources report three Palestinians killed Sunday in Khan Younis.

What happened

This is a developing-story monitor. The thread: Gaza ceasefire diplomacy continues, with Jared Kushner cautioning that 'hard work' lies ahead. Awaiting verification: the specific contents of the current round of talks, the parties' positions, and any timetable.

What remains disputed

Nearly everything material: who proposed what, whether the gaps are closing, and what 'hard work ahead' refers to in concrete terms. No primary documents or on-record statements beyond the reported Kushner remark have been established here. Palestinian casualty figures (74,000+ killed) are Palestinian-authority figures, attributed but not independently verified. The Shujaiya advance and the drone-strike casualty details rest on hospital and local sources without Israeli comment. The Health Ministry's cumulative figure differs across outlets (1,488 via Tasnim vs 1,493 via L'Orient Today); kept as a range until one ministry figure is confirmed.

Why it matters

Gaza diplomacy and the Iran war are now linked pressure systems. A Gaza ceasefire would change the regional temperature — and the Houthi/Saudi/Yemen escalations show the cost of failure.

Strategic context

Months of on-and-off Gaza negotiations have repeatedly stalled at the same obstacles: hostages, withdrawal terms, and post-war governance. The current round is being watched for whether regional pressure from the Iran war finally forces movement.

Competing interpretations

Reading one: real momentum, with the Iran war creating incentives for a deal. Reading two: managed optics — talks as process, not progress. The evidence is not yet sufficient to choose.

Treasury license would let Russian diesel flow through April 2027

The diesel deal's sanctions carve-out reportedly extends through April 2027 — turning a wartime fix into a year-plus arrangement.

Updated 17 Oct 2026, 05:36 UTC Verified 4 sources
Full paper https://times-of-the-world.vercel.app/treasury-license-russian-diesel/

What happened

This is a developing-story monitor. The thread: the Treasury license underpinning the Trump–Putin diesel arrangement reportedly runs through April 2027 — well past the US midterms and deep into the war's expected runway. Awaiting verification: the license text itself.

What is verified

  • OFAC general license authorizes Russian-diesel sale/delivery/offloading/import through 12:01 am EDT, April 7, 2027 (The Hindu/PTI, Oct 10, 2026).
  • The license excludes debits to accounts of Russia's central bank, national wealth fund, and finance ministry at US institutions (The National, Oct 9, 2026).
  • Trump described delivery phases totaling ~4.8M tons nominal (Truth Social via The Hindu/PTI/USA Today; exact phasing varies across outlets).
  • The diesel deal includes a temporary US sanctions lift on Russian fuel (Haaretz, Oct 10, 2026).

What remains disputed

The exact expiry date, the scope of products covered, renewal terms, and whether Congress or allies were consulted. An 18-month carve-out is a different animal than a 90-day emergency waiver — the difference matters.

Why it matters

Duration is the story. A short waiver is crisis management; a license through April 2027 is a de facto sanctions regime change for Russian refined products. It would give Moscow a year-plus revenue channel and give US fuel markets a year-plus of Russian supply — with everything that implies for Ukraine policy and European coordination.

Strategic context

US sanctions on Russian energy have been the West's central economic weapon since 2022. A long-dated license punches a hole in that regime — and hands Moscow leverage: every renewal date becomes a negotiation.

Competing interpretations

Reading one: pragmatic — markets need certainty, and a long license prevents a price cliff. Reading two: this is normalization by the back door, executed without a public policy debate. Reading three: the date may be a ceiling, not a commitment — licenses can be revoked.

What changed since last update

GRAHAM ACT WAIVER — Trump signed an executive order waiving the application of the sanctions, restrictions and duties in the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026 (100%-duty authority over major buyers of Russian oil and gas), certifying to Congress that the waiver is in US national interests; the full verified ledger is on st-001 (multi-outlet). This is a separate legal instrument from the OFAC diesel general license through April 7, 2027 already on this stack — the waiver clears the newly legislated sanctions layer; the OFAC carve-out governs the diesel transactions themselves. THE LICENSE TEXT IS OUT — OFAC's general license authorizes all transactions prohibited by the Russian Harmful Foreign Activities Sanctions Regulations related to the sale, delivery, offloading or importation (including into the US) of Russian-origin diesel through 12:01 am EDT, April 7, 2027 (The Hindu/PTI, Oct 10). The license does NOT authorize debits to accounts of Russia's central bank, national wealth fund or finance ministry at US financial institutions (The National, Oct 9). Trump on Truth Social described phases — 300,000+ tons immediately, 500,000 tons in November, 1 million tons immediately thereafter, then 3 million tons more conditional on refinery condition (~4.8M tons nominal total; USA Today reports the November tranche as 1M tons — phasing differs across outlets and is treated as reported, not settled). monitor opened on the license-duration thread.

What to watch next

  • Publication of the actual Treasury/OFAC license text.
  • European reaction — does Brussels follow or object?
  • Ukrainian government response.
  • Whether the license covers only diesel or wider refined products.
  • Whether Brussels objects to or mirrors the carve-out for Russian refined products.

Russia is supplying missiles to Iran as Tehran rebuilds its arsenal, report says

Iran retains production capacity and substantial ballistic missile stocks despite eight months of US-Israeli airstrikes, according to Bloomberg reporting cited by Haaretz.

What happened

Russia is supplying Iran with missiles as Tehran rebuilds its arsenal, according to Bloomberg reporting cited by Haaretz on October 10, 2026. The reporting states Iran retains production capacity and substantial ballistic missile stocks despite eight months of US-Israeli airstrikes.

What is verified

  • Bloomberg reporting, cited by Haaretz (Oct 10, 2026), says Russia is supplying missiles to Iran.
  • The reporting states Iran retains ballistic missile production capacity and substantial stocks.
  • US-Israeli airstrikes against Iran have been ongoing for roughly eight months (since Feb 28, 2026).
  • Western intelligence assessed in June that Iran retained ~75% of its prewar arsenal (Bloomberg reporting, carried by Newsmax/NewsBeep, Oct 10, 2026).
  • Britain's navy says at least 10 vessels were struck near Hormuz in the past week; oil above $100/barrel; tanker charters ~$1.2M/day (same).
  • Iran reportedly test-fired multiple ballistic missiles from Tehran and Alborz provinces on Sunday Oct 11 (Türkiye Today, Oct 11, 2026; C14 News, Oct 11, 2026).
  • Bloomberg Economics analysis, carried by Türkiye Today (Oct 11, 2026), says Iran has launched more than 4,000 Shahed drones and at least 2,000 ballistic missiles since Feb 28.
  • Trump said late Monday he does not believe China or Russia are helping Iran 'at all,' telling Truth Social they have 'too much to lose' (Reuters, Oct 13, 2026) — on-record statement contradicting the Bloomberg assessment.
  • IDF raised alert level across all fronts amid reports Iran launched 30 ballistic missiles (Israel Hayom, Oct 15 — single outlet, attributed)
  • Israeli security officials: no change in Iran intelligence assessment justifying an operation in the next 2-3 weeks; Iran's missile rebuilding slow and partial (Times of Israel/Channel 12, Oct 15 — reported)

What remains disputed

The types, quantities, and delivery timeline of the missiles have not been publicly specified. The claim that eight months of strikes failed to degrade Iran's missile capability will be contested by US and Israeli officials; no official US/Israeli assessment is reflected in the cited reporting. Russia's strategic intent — defensive resupply vs. deepening war partnership — is unstated.

Why it matters

If accurate, this is the clearest evidence yet that the US-Israeli air campaign has not achieved its stated aim of degrading Iran's strike capability — and that Moscow is willing to backfill it. That combination hardens Iran's negotiating position and lengthens the war's expected runway.

Strategic context

Russia and Iran deepened military cooperation after 2022, most visibly through Iranian drones in Ukraine. The reverse flow — Russian munitions into Iran — marks a new phase. It follows the Putin–Pezeshkian meeting in Ashgabat, where Moscow pledged mediation while visibly tightening its embrace of Tehran.

Competing interpretations

Reading one: this is routine alliance maintenance — Moscow resupplying a partner to keep the war grinding and keep Washington distracted. Reading two: it is a deterrent signal to Washington and Jerusalem ahead of any escalation. Reading three (skeptical): the reporting may overstate deliveries or conflate agreements with delivered hardware; the physical evidence is not public.
