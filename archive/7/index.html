Treasury license would let Russian diesel flow through April 2027

The diesel deal's sanctions carve-out reportedly extends through April 2027 — turning a wartime fix into a year-plus arrangement.

Updated 27 Oct 2026, 03:34 UTC Verified 14 sources
Full paper https://times-of-the-world.vercel.app/treasury-license-russian-diesel/

What happened

This is a developing-story monitor. The thread: the Treasury license underpinning the Trump–Putin diesel arrangement reportedly runs through April 2027 — well past the US midterms and deep into the war's expected runway. Awaiting verification: the license text itself.

What is verified

  • OFAC general license authorizes Russian-diesel sale/delivery/offloading/import through 12:01 am EDT, April 7, 2027 (The Hindu/PTI, Oct 10, 2026).
  • The license excludes debits to accounts of Russia's central bank, national wealth fund, and finance ministry at US institutions (The National, Oct 9, 2026).
  • Trump described delivery phases totaling ~4.8M tons nominal (Truth Social via The Hindu/PTI/USA Today; exact phasing varies across outlets).
  • The diesel deal includes a temporary US sanctions lift on Russian fuel (Haaretz, Oct 10, 2026).
  • Trump signed a Section 115 waiver of the Graham Act duties/sanctions Oct 16-17 Washington time, just as the 30-day implementation deadline expired; order text certifies the waiver 'is in the national interests of the United States' (SCMP/Bloomberg/ANI/Tribune/TOI, Oct 17 — VERIFIED)
  • Waived: duties under Sections 112 and 113 (up to 500% ad valorem on Russian goods, up to 100% on five largest importers of Russian crude/gas); sanctions/restrictions under Section 102(d) and other provisions (TOI — VERIFIED); the levies remain formally imposed, so Trump could re-apply them (SCMP — attributed legal analysis)
  • Secretary of State Marco Rubio said President Trump would use up to 60 days to assess the economic consequences of the waiver on key sanctions and tariffs under the Graham Act (Reuters via Times of India / Economic Times / Straits Times, Oct 19 — on-record statement)
  • Trump said Russia would immediately supply more than 300,000 tonnes of diesel to the US and global markets, followed by 500,000 tonnes in November and 1 million tonnes after that, plus a further 3 million tonnes depending on the condition of Russian refineries (Oil & Gas Journal — reported, attributed)
  • Delivery remains uncertain: Ukrainian strikes have damaged Russian refineries, and Moscow's diesel export restrictions remain partly in place despite easing on October 10 (same — attributed)
  • Deputy PM Alexander Novak said Wednesday the first tanker carrying 40,000 metric tons of Russian diesel for the US departs Thursday, October 22; fuel paperwork nearing completion; further shipments to follow (Vedomosti/RIA via The Hindu BusinessLine and Ukrainska Pravda, Oct 21 — on-record statement, VERIFIED)
  • European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen said Wednesday morning the EU will not ease sanctions on Russian fossil fuels despite the US diesel deal (Politico, Oct 21 — on-record remarks, VERIFIED)
  • Dmitriev (Oct 22): first 40,000-ton diesel tanker sailed Thursday for the US under the Trump-Putin deal — first Russian fuel shipment to the US since 2022 (VERIFIED as his announcement; independent sailing confirmation not yet in hand) (The USA New Times).
  • Bipartisan Senate/House letters sent Saturday warn the CEOs of six major banks and the heads of the Houston, Corpus Christi, Beaumont and New York/New Jersey port authorities that facilitating Russian-diesel imports may expose them to criminal and civil liability under 18 U.S.C. \u00a7 545 and/or 19 U.S.C. \u00a7 1595a, demand preservation of all related records and a written confirmation by October 28, 2026 (Senate Foreign Relations Committee release, Oct 26 — VERIFIED as the letters; IANS, Oct 27, second publisher). The letters' legal reading is attributed, not a court finding; no administration, bank or port response appears in the material reviewed.

What remains disputed

The exact expiry date, the scope of products covered, renewal terms, and whether Congress or allies were consulted. An 18-month carve-out is a different animal than a 90-day emergency waiver — the difference matters. The legal validity of OFAC General License No. 135 is now openly contested: House Judiciary Ranking Member Raskin argues (Oct 26) it cannot override the 2022 statutory ban; no court ruling on the license's validity appears in the material reviewed.

Why it matters

Duration is the story. A short waiver is crisis management; a license through April 2027 is a de facto sanctions regime change for Russian refined products. It would give Moscow a year-plus revenue channel and give US fuel markets a year-plus of Russian supply — with everything that implies for Ukraine policy and European coordination.

Strategic context

US sanctions on Russian energy have been the West's central economic weapon since 2022. A long-dated license punches a hole in that regime — and hands Moscow leverage: every renewal date becomes a negotiation.

Competing interpretations

Reading one: pragmatic — markets need certainty, and a long license prevents a price cliff. Reading two: this is normalization by the back door, executed without a public policy debate. Reading three: the date may be a ceiling, not a commitment — licenses can be revoked.

US sanctions the entire ICC — Rubio vows to dismantle it 'brick by brick'

The US sanctioned the Hague court as an institution on Oct 9, hours after a former ICC judge won the Nobel Peace Prize — demanding the Netanyahu/Gallant warrants be dropped and issuing an April 2027 ultimatum. Canada, France, Germany, Japan and the UK rejected the move.

What happened

This is a developing-story monitor. The thread: the US, with Secretary of State Marco Rubio as the public face, is moving to sanction the International Criminal Court. Awaiting verification: the specific designations, the legal instrument used, and the stated rationale.

What remains disputed

The targets, the legal basis, allied reaction, and whether this is tied to the Iran war, the Gaza file, or a broader institutional confrontation — all unestablished.

Why it matters

Sanctioning the court that investigates war crimes — while the US is itself a party to a war — is a statement about the rules-based order as much as about the ICC. Allies who back the court will be forced to choose.

Strategic context

US–ICC friction is not new; it spans administrations. What is new is the context: an active US war, an ICC with live files on the region, and an administration comfortable using sanctions as a multi-front tool.

Competing interpretations

Reading one: defensive — preempting ICC scrutiny of the Iran war. Reading two: ideological — a broader assault on international institutions constraining US action. Reading three: leverage — sanctions as a bargaining chip for something else entirely.

The other finalists: Tanzanian opposition leader Tundu Lissu (EPP/ECR-backed) and Iranian twin sisters Taraneh and Romina Rahimi, imprisoned in Iran (Patriots for Europe-backed). The ICC candidacy was put forward by the parliament's Socialists and Democrats (S&D) group; centrist MEP Barry Andrews framed the shortlisting as support 'for a fraying rules-based international order' (Dawn, Oct 20 — VERIFIED). The final laureate is chosen Thursday, Oct 22, by the Conference of Presidents, with the ceremony Dec 16 in Strasbourg. BLOCKING-STATUTE PUSH — the day before, MEPs on the Foreign Affairs committee adopted (50-18, 4 abstentions) a report on protecting international justice calling for the EU's blocking statute to be activated and for European banks, insurers and digital providers to keep serving sanctioned court staff and organisations (Agence Europe, Oct 20 — VERIFIED; committee vote, not plenary law). Human-rights subcommittee chair Mounir Satouri (Greens/EFA) criticized the Commission's Kaja Kallas for inaction (same — ATTRIBUTED). EU EYES BLOCKING STATUTE — the European Commission said Monday it is considering measures, including activating its blocking statute, to protect the International Criminal Court from US sanctions; Commission spokesperson Siobhan McGarry told a Brussels midday briefing that 'the blocking statute still remains on the table', with preparatory work underway but no decision made on whether or when to activate it; the Commission is consulting member states and ICC state parties on practical measures, with the Dutch leading (TRT World / Anadolu — VERIFIED as official Commission briefing). LEGAL BODIES CONDEMN SANCTIONS — the Law Society of Scotland, the Faculty of Advocates and 15 other legal organisations jointly condemned the US sanctions against the International Criminal Court, warning they risk undermining investigations, trials and victims' access to justice; the signatories called on the US administration to withdraw all sanctions and urged member states to use diplomatic means to secure their reversal (scottishlegal.com, Oct 19 — on-record joint statement). EU WEIGHS BLOCKING STATUTE TO SHIELD ICC — the European Commission said Monday it is considering measures, including activating its blocking statute, to protect the International Criminal Court from the US sanctions: Commission spokesperson Siobhan McGarry told the midday Brussels briefing the EU has been in contact with member states over potential activation following Washington's announcement (Anadolu Agency, Brussels dateline, Oct 19 — VERIFIED as her on-record briefing statement; single-carrier at build time). McGarry said preparatory work is underway but stressed no decision has been made: 'The blocking statute still remains on the table'; 'We have no definitive date or time for the activation, or we won't prejudge whether we will activate it. We're obviously still hoping that we can find a more optimal outcome to allow the court to continue its vital work' (same — VERIFIED as statement). The statute is designed to protect EU companies and individuals from complying with certain foreign sanctions; McGarry said the Commission is also consulting several ICC state parties on practical measures to protect court operations, with the Netherlands 'very, very much leading on this work' (same). First institutional countermove to the sanctions since Rubio's Oct 9 announcement (v1-v6). Builds on v3's US court injunction. LICENSE MECHANICS CLARIFIED — OFAC's designation comes with General License 13, which authorizes specified maintenance and wind-down transactions through 12:01 a.m. EDT on April 7, 2027; License 14 separately permits specified telecommunications and enterprise-software transactions without that stated deadline; Licenses 15 and 16 address pension payments and certain detainees, with no stated expiry (Chatham House, Oct 15; The Dispute Index — two independent analyses of the OFAC action; the 180-day/april-2027 wind-down window corroborates the 'six-month timeline' in v1). The licenses limit the immediate bite of the institutional designation while leaving the earlier individual designations — judges, prosecutor — in place (same sources). Cross-linked to the Furman injunction (v5), which blocks enforcement against two rights groups. JUDGE FURMAN BLOCKS ENFORCEMENT AGAINST TWO RIGHTS GROUPS — SDNY Judge Jesse M. Furman 'granted a preliminary injunction in a 30-page order' (Oct 13; reported Oct 14-15), finding the groups likely to succeed on First Amendment claims; the ban on providing services to sanctioned ICC-related persons 'constituted a content-based regulation of their speech-based activities and did not survive strict scrutiny.' Bars federal agencies from enforcing civil fines 'up to $377,700' or criminal penalties 'up to 20 years in prison' under IEEPA against plaintiffs DAWN and TAAG, which announced they will resume submitting war-crimes evidence to the court (JPost/Anadolu/Mail & Guardian/political.org — VERIFIED). Furman 'rejected the plaintiffs' request to declare the 2025 executive order as facially unconstitutional' — EO 14203 remains in place; the injunction does not address the Oct 9 institutional designation. The first judicial wound in the sanctions campaign since Rubio's Oct 9 institutional designation — a narrow, plaintiff-specific crack, not a demolition. EU COUNCIL SUMMIT DECLARATION (OCT 16-17) — EU leaders 'regretted US sanctions against the International Criminal Court (ICC), urging Washington to reverse the measures and reaffirming the EU's readiness to protect the court and ensure its continued access to financial and other services' (Daily Sabah, Oct 16; MENAFN, Oct 17 — STRONGLY_SUPPORTED; no primary consilium text fetched). The first leader-level, institution-wide countermeasure pledge — aimed squarely at neutralizing the sanctions' stated purpose ('crippling its ability to operate'); pairs with Dutch PM Jetten's 'we will do whatever we need to defend the ICC'. ATHENS: CONTESTED — Rapporteur (~Oct 14, exclusive): 'Rubio sought Greece's support in the Trump administration's campaign against the ICC during his recent visit to Athens... Rubio would like Greece to withdraw from the Rome Statute' — one unnamed diplomat; vs. Gerapetritis (Oct 17): ICC 'was not discussed during his talks in Athens with Marco Rubio'; 'Athens Backs ICC Independence, Urges US to Reconsider Sanctions' (tovima/euagenda — CONTESTED on whether Rubio raised it). The two stories cannot both be true — Gerapetritis's public denial, issued three days after the leak, favors the latter interpretation but does not settle it. What is stable: Athens publicly aligned with the EU consensus line. PILLAY HITS BACK (OCT 16) — 'He [Trump] should call in his advisors to tell him why we say that it is unlawful for him to interfere with an independent international court.' / 'You can't selectively pursue one lot of people and not the other. It will be such a bad, dangerous precedent.' (The Citizen South Africa, opinion format — ATTRIBUTED; single outlet, needs a second before established). VAN SCHAACK: GRACE PERIOD IS A TRAP (OCT 16) — former US Ambassador-at-Large for Global Criminal Justice Beth van Schaack to JusticeInfo: 'States [parties to the ICC] may attempt to make certain concessions to this administration, sacrificing their values or dignity in the process, only to find in the end that this administration will not budge an inch.' / 'There is very little to negotiate... if anything, it may throw them under a bus further.' (ANALYSIS/ATTRIBUTED). PILLAY: "ILLEGAL" SANCTIONS — Nobel laureate Navi Pillay told AFP on Oct 14 that Trump's sanctions against the ICC are "illegal," that the president needed a lesson in international law ("Call in your legal advisors to explain to you what international law is"), and that the sanctions' timing — announced hours after her Nobel — was a "reaction" to the award not going to Trump; she said prior US administrations welcomed an ICC arrest warrant against Putin and called the current posture selective double standards (Times of Israel / IOL via AFP, Oct 14-15 — VERIFIED as on-record statements). OCT 20 DEADLINE — the parties in the Furman injunction litigation have until Oct 20 to address the next procedural steps, including a possible permanent injunction (Mail & Guardian, Oct 15 — reported); the injunction remains temporary and protects only the plaintiffs. WIND-DOWN — a legal analysis notes EO 14203's asset freezes and transaction bans carry limited exceptions: the ongoing detention of some awaiting trial, pension payments, IT and social-media use, and a 180-day "wind-down" permitting certain otherwise-prohibited transactions until April 2027 (almendron, Oct 16 — attributed analysis). FEDERAL JUDGE BLOCKS ICC SANCTIONS AS APPLIED TO TWO US RIGHTS GROUPS — US District Judge Jesse Furman issued a temporary injunction blocking the Trump administration's ICC sanctions as applied to two American rights organizations — DAWN and TAAG — that aid the ICC's Gaza war-crimes inquiry (political.org, Oct 14; countercurrents.org, Oct 15). The challenge targets Executive Order 14203 (signed Feb 6, 2025); the order itself remains in place. Furman ruled that, as applied to the plaintiffs, the ban on providing 'services' to designated persons regulates speech based on content and cannot satisfy strict scrutiny (First Amendment); the groups also invoked the Fifth Amendment's due process clause. DAWN said it regularly provides the ICC evidence of alleged war crimes under Article 15 of the Rome Statute; TAAG had suspended its planned cooperation out of fear of prosecution (same). The injunction is temporary and protects only the plaintiffs while the suit proceeds — it does not strike down the broader sanctions framework (same). Two outlets carrying; keep as reported pending wire confirmation. THE SANCTIONS ARE REAL — the US on Oct 9 sanctioned the ICC as an institution (the entire tribunal, for the first time), adding it to the Treasury's list of sanctioned entities and moving to ban transactions with the court, with a six-month wind-down/grace period for businesses dealing with it (AP via Military.com, Oct 9; CNA, Oct 10; Le Monde, Oct 10). Rubio's demands: cancel the Nov 21, 2024 arrest warrants against Netanyahu and Gallant; close the investigation into US military actions in Afghanistan and CIA secret prisons; reform the Rome Statute to bar prosecution of nationals of non-party states (Le Monde, Oct 10). Full effect by April 9, 2027 unless the court complies — Rubio: 'Either the ICC will end its threats, or we will end the ICC' (Le Monde). The announcement came hours after the Nobel Peace Prize went to former ICC judge Navanethem 'Navi' Pillay — the move long predated the prize, AP notes (CNA; USA Today). Pushback: the ICC rejected the sanctions, warning 'history will judge this moment'; President Akane Tomoko called them 'historically unprecedented' (NHK); UN chief Guterres called them 'a serious blow to our collective pursuit of international criminal justice' (CNA); Canada, France, Germany, Japan and the UK said in a joint statement they 'strongly disagree' (CNA). RUBIO PRESSES THE ICC LINE WITH JAPAN — US Secretary of State Marco Rubio raised what a State Department readout called 'the necessity of U.S. sanctions on the International Criminal Court' in a weekend phone call with Japanese Foreign Minister Toshimitsu Motegi, describing the sanctions as needed because of the Court's 'rogue actions' threatening American sovereignty (Japan Times, Oct 11). A Japanese Foreign Ministry readout released late Saturday said Motegi 'conveyed the Japanese government's position on the recent U.S. institutional sanctions,' with the two agreeing 'to maintain close communication' — a softer line that keeps Tokyo inside the eight-country joint statement rejecting the sanctions (v5). Two readouts, two framings; no new US policy move — Washington's sanctions stand, with the 180-day licensing grace period (v5) leaving the practical fight for the coming weeks. monitor opened.
